Coding with agents has turned the act of coding into an act of steering using prompts, enabling us to comfortably navigate complex codebases, fix problems in unfamiliar corners, and build cool new features in no time. This capability is also available to a lot more people - dare I say, everyone? We live in a world of exciting possibilities!

And yet, a few days of building something new from scratch left me scratching my head when a workflow I was building looked alien to me.

When Your Own Code Looks Foreign

It all started when testing revealed some unexpected behavior. In this case, the number of LLM calls the workflow made, and their duration, looked off. This forced me to peek under the cover and the abstractions looked very odd. Each LLM turn was designed to be a lot more limited than I had imagined.

Despite crafting this system using my own specs and guidance, I had failed to catch functionally correct but directionally inefficient choices my coding agent had made for me.

Looking back, I can see how I had presumed there is exactly one efficient way of solving the problem as described, and my agent, in all its wisdom, will obviously choose that. While I was reviewing every commit with excitement at the start, a combination of review fatigue and a false sense of security had lowered my guard.

This made me wonder - if it’s so easy to lose track of the critical details of the systems we build within days, what are the many things we are no longer learning because the agent is doing the work for us?

The Debt We Don’t See

For years, maturing engineering teams have grappled with technical debt while striving to balance top-line business priorities against this bottom-line line item.

Poor abstractions, hurriedly taken shortcuts, a stack of whack-a-mole patches - each making future changes to the codebase harder than the last one. As this technical debt accumulated with a relentless march, most engineers pictured it as a squall of broken windows that they were not proud of but learned to grudgingly live with.

Snap forward to today and most of us aren’t even reading the code our agents are generating, let alone trying to reason about it. We seem to have augmented technical debt with a greater ill: Cognitive Debt.

Technical debt lives in the system itself. We could even point at it because it often got in the way. Cognitive debt lives within the mental models each engineer holds about the system they own. It forces them to evolve, run and maintain the system with an, at best, incomplete understanding of how it all comes together.

Where The Reps Went

Because it was just how we went about our work, we rarely stopped to think about how we developed the skills to reason about a software system in the first place. Now that we have a contrasting setup, the answer is loud and clear.

Writing and debugging code may be seen to be tedious work today, but it forced engineers to practice:

  • Decomposition: Tearing a system down to its parts and then stitching it back up

  • Code Literacy: The ability to read someone else’s code and reason about it

  • Hypothesising: Forming possible explanations or educated guesses about an unknown using facts and data

  • Mental Simulation: Developing and holding a working model of the system in your brain

  • Isolation: Systematically eliminating hypotheses using tests, data and reasoning to identify the most likely root-cause

With agents now performing most of these steps, we have relieved ourselves of the repetition that actually created these instincts in the first place.

Senior engineers built up a reservoir of these repetitions over the years. The scar tissue this left behind still shows up as their “spidey sense” which is triggered when something just looks off.

An engineer entering the industry while agents are taking hold will be deprived of the very training stimulus that develops these instincts.

How are we going to make sure the next generation of engineers will retain the edge required to reason about systems at large?

The Black Box is Insufficient

Now, one may argue that engineers no longer need to understand the inner workings of a system to keep the lights on. They only need to understand the intended behavior of a black box model of the system to guide an agent to debug and fix it.

On your simplified, single-tenant local setup, this tends to work well. Code tells the agent what could happen. Observability (logs, metrics, traces) tells it what did happen.

Sadly, debugging something in production is nothing like this. Any suitably involved production system will have distributed state, multiple concurrent transactions, and asynchronous workflows. You also have external dependencies, even if it’s just a service maintained by another team, and retries, caches, intermittent failure patterns around them.

There are simply too many things that could go wrong. A suitably powerful agent can investigate by forming and testing hypotheses, much like a human might. Without a mental model of the system, the agent could brute force its way through all possible paths, but end up prematurely converging on something that fits the data available.

This search gets considerably more efficient and reliable when a human with a working mental model of the system steers it toward what is plausible, surprising or worth investigating first.

Make Understanding Part of “Done”

If systems reasoning and a working mental model are critical tools for engineers to develop or retain, how do we nurture the learning loops that foster them?

While the exact process will change across teams, the essence lies in an engineer being held accountable for explaining every change they prepare. The idea is not to be able to explain every line, but the crux of the solution itself. This includes:

  • the alternatives considered

  • the important trade-offs

  • key assumptions

  • the failure modes considered

  • the chosen approach and why it was chosen

The challenge is to formalize this without slowing the agentic pipeline down. Even if you can only apply this check intermittently, it is critical to inculcate a culture where

You are allowed to delegate implementation. You cannot delegate understanding of the decision.

Follow It Into Production

The best way to appreciate the results of the choices you made (or enabled the agent to make for you) is to watch your code in production.

When code meets the messiness of the real world and the scale and complexity of a production deployment, it is being tested in the environment that matters to your users.

In fact, this test cannot be an afterthought. You are expected to ask the following while you are still preparing the change:

  1. What do I expect to happen when this change receives real traffic?

  2. How will I know?

This is not simply about imagining failure modes and handling them gracefully. Once a change is in production, you should be able to follow it to see how it behaves.

  • Study the metrics

  • Compare actual vs expected behavior

  • Investigate surprises

  • Record and share what you learned

This is how you ensure a system was observable and does what it was designed for, while exercising the muscles that build your working mental model of the system.

Retain The Useful Friction

Agentic tools thrive on the idea of removing friction. But not all friction was unwelcome. Friction tends to bring focus and activates important attention centers that enable learning.

Reintroducing some friction strategically might help individuals and teams build the very skills we are worried they are losing at the wrong time.

Being able to explain your changes during a review is one we already touched upon. Other practices you should consider adding to your team’s workflow include:

  • Architecture walkthroughs after substantial changes

  • Incident ownership by the person responsible for the change

  • Team-wide sessions for individuals to share recent lessons

  • Team-wide reviews of incidents and their RCAs

Without eating into the productivity gains fostered by agents, this kind of friction can help ensure efficiency did not come at the cost of eliminating reflection, reasoning and apprenticeship on your team.

Build Capability, Not Just Output

As agents help teams ship more and the whole marketing around this technology screams hyper-productivity, it is natural for leaders to get caught up in optimising for output. In fact, focusing on efficiency and output isn’t new to leaders or their teams. The only difference is that this seems to have become all-consuming.

The short term gains of productivity must be balanced with the longer term needs of your team, which will involve dealing with high-severity incidents, urgent customer requests, messy migrations, scaling alongside growth, and increasingly so, multi-pronged cyberattacks.

This requires producing engineers who can reason about systems, make long-lasting technical judgments, diagnose ambiguous failures, challenge assumptions and eventually become the senior engineers the organization’s future will come to depend on.

If we are systematically delegating away all the parts that demand reasoning and systems thinking, we may improve today’s throughput while stealing from our teams the very opportunities that train them to succeed with those longer term needs.

The real leadership challenge, then, is not simply to ask how much more software a team can produce with AI. It is to ask whether the team is becoming more capable while doing it.
