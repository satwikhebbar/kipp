A learning substrate for Kipp

A lay of the land for making agents improve from feedback, overrides, published work, and outcomes—without fine-tuning. The recommendation is a composable, evidence-first substrate: it improves the context and policies supplied at runtime, not a hidden global prompt.

1. Executive framing

Kipp’s problem is not “give an LLM a bigger memory.” It is to turn observations into bounded, attributable, and retrievable runtime context that improves a later decision. A Telegram correction, an unexplained override, a published-post delta, and engagement metrics are different evidence types; they must not be collapsed into one permanent preference.

Working conclusion: keep minimal stable instructions and make learning a separate, versioned context layer. Preserve raw observations; consolidate them into scoped lessons with confidence; retrieve only those relevant to the present task; measure the result; then strengthen, weaken, supersede, or retire the lesson. Fixed safety controls—not an LLM—continue to decide credential access, publishing approval, budgets, and routing.

Position on the 2023 foundations: CoALA and Generative Agents are retained as useful origins for the separation of memory, action, observation, reflection, and retrieval; they are not presented as the current state of the art. Recent surveys such as Rethinking Memory Mechanisms of Foundation Agents and Graph-based Agent Memory, plus the 2026 evaluation work below, add user-centric memory, lifecycle operations, temporal change, cost/freshness, and rigorous evaluation. Those modern additions drive this memo’s recommendation.

Non-goals for the first substrate: fine-tuning, autonomous publishing, a universal agent framework, or binding the design to today’s LinkedIn and Calendar workflows. Engagement is a secondary outcome signal, not a stand-in for author intent: when authorized it can mean impressions, reactions, comments, reshares, clicks, or an engagement rate over a declared window. It must be evaluated alongside author acceptance, never substituted for it.

2. Requirements and evaluation rubric

Three proof cases

  1. Explicit feedback: the author directly explains an expectation, preference, decision rationale, or desired change—whether before a draft or while revising one. Repeated explanations can become a source-linked, scoped lesson that automatically improves a later relevant draft.
  2. Implicit override: an unexplained replacement is weak evidence. Kipp may compare the versions, record a tentative possible lesson, or ask later; it may not silently create a global hard rule.
  3. Asynchronous published outcome: Kipp can ingest an author-approved live post, compare it with the earlier draft where available, and attach metrics/comments as outcome evidence for a future topic-relevant draft.

4. Recent research scan: 29 January–29 July 2026

This supplement corrects an initial gap in the memo. The foundational papers elsewhere explain the origin of the patterns; this section records a targeted six-month scan of primary research on agent memory, non-parametric continual improvement, long-horizon evaluation, and memory-system behaviour. Most results are recent preprints, not yet evidence of production readiness. The 2026 surveys cited above support the same conclusion: the old memory/reflection vocabulary remains useful, but modern systems need explicit memory lifecycles, user/task scope, and evaluation—not a generic reflection prompt.

What changed because of this scan: the recommendation now has stronger recent support for temporal supersession, context-budget selection, interference testing, controlled transfer evaluation, and a separate background consolidation path. It does not change the initial recommendation against fine-tuning or unreviewable autonomous memory policies. The literature’s strongest current interventions often train a memory policy; Kipp can first implement the corresponding observable policy as data and fixed code.

Canonical data model, minimally: Observation (immutable raw event plus its task/conversation context), EvidenceLink (draft ↔ published artefact/diff/metric), Lesson (a consolidated, confidence-scored claim with scope, status, and evidence IDs), ContextPackage (lesson/example IDs selected for a run), Outcome (human edit, explicit score, metric snapshot), and DecisionTrace (run/version references). Embeddings, graphs, and third-party trace products are indexes or views, never the only copy.

7. Applied walkthroughs

A. Explicit Telegram feedback

  1. Store the author’s explanation verbatim as an observation, attached to the draft, author, conversation, task goal, topic, audience, and relevant trace ID.
  2. Consolidation detects repetition across comparable posts and records a confidence-scored lesson learned: “For technical posts aimed at peers, prefer a concrete opening before an abstract claim,” with evidence IDs and an applicability scope.
  3. On a later technical-draft request, filtered retrieval selects that lesson and one or two approved examples. The context package states why each item is present.
  4. Draft generation applies a sufficiently confident lesson automatically. A human edit, explanation, or later outcome attaches to this exact package, allowing its confidence and scope to be recalibrated.

B. Unexplained override

  1. Preserve both proposed and replacement text plus the full surrounding context; mark the reason unknown.
  2. Run a bounded diff/interpreter job that can propose several possible lessons, each explicitly labelled weak and none globally active.
  3. Only strengthen one when corroborated by further edits, explicit feedback, or a later targeted question. Until then it may be retrieved only as a low-priority “consider” example—or not at all.
  4. Evaluate two distinct north-star outcomes: whether the human accepts/edits a draft more readily, and whether comparable accepted posts have stronger engagement. Never claim an edit itself caused engagement, and never let either outcome replace the other.

C. Published LinkedIn post and metrics

  1. User supplies a post URL or authorized export; Kipp creates a PublishedPost record with the canonical text, author identity, publication time, post URN/URL, and metrics only through approved access.
  2. Before publishing or exporting, the LinkedIn workflow writes the draft’s draftId, conversationId, workflowRunId, DecisionTrace ID, and ContextPackage ID. The EvidenceLink then joins that bundle to the PublishedPost; a manual import offers the author a search/select step when no direct link exists.
  3. Derive the draft-to-published delta as evidence. If no prior Kipp draft exists, treat the post as an example, not proof of a lesson.
  4. Snapshot metrics over a declared window and normalize only for comparison candidates that share sufficient context. Comments may be input evidence, not a reason to imitate commenters. Consolidation can then form a topic/style example and a weak outcome association; retrieval uses topic, audience, intent, age, and author scope—not “highest engagement wins.”
Automatic application remains compatible with caution: once a lesson reaches its declared confidence threshold, Kipp automatically supplies it in a context package. Caution applies to forming and strengthening lessons, not to repeatedly asking the user to activate known preferences.

8. Fit with a durable, long-running future workflow

This is not required to make a LinkedIn draft learn today. Durable workflows are included because proactive learning eventually needs background observation, delayed metric collection, and consolidation that survives a retry or a wait for a human. A future Kipp workflow may discover sources, call several services, pause for days, receive webhooks, resume after deployment, branch into investigation and action, and be interrupted by the user. The learning substrate must be an independent service contract around that lifecycle—not a LinkedIn post-processing script.

Tomorrow’s example: a research-and-outreach workflow

Kipp receives a goal to prepare outreach after monitoring an industry change. It periodically observes approved sources, detects a change, gathers evidence with several tools, drafts an approach using author-specific communication lessons, waits for a human response, receives a correction, and later observes whether the target replied. The durable runtime owns waits/retries and the workflow’s state machine. The learning substrate owns evidence, context packages, and outcome-linked hypotheses. A tool guard, not learned memory, decides whether a message can ever be sent.

9. Recommended starting position

Build a thin Kipp-owned evidence core first. Use the existing Cloudflare path only for background consolidation that needs it; make a relational store the source of truth; start with filtered lexical/metadata retrieval and add embeddings only when a small evaluation set shows they improve context selection. Instrument the learning loop using OpenTelemetry-compatible traces (Phoenix or Langfuse are the leading candidates). Treat Graphiti, Letta, Supermemory, and Inngest as design inputs, not foundational dependencies.
  1. Define contracts, not a universal agent: observations, confidence-scored lessons, context packages, outcomes, and fixed safety boundaries.
  2. Implement explicit-feedback learning first: it has the strongest signal and an immediate end-to-end evaluation loop.
  3. Add implicit override as evidence-only: capture diffs and possible lessons, but do not auto-promote from a single unexplained override.
  4. Add published-post ingestion by explicit URL/export: save the draft/conversation/run/context-package linkage, then validate the evidence and lifecycle before pursuing restricted member API access.
  5. Build an evaluation set from real, consented historical cases: compare no learning, raw examples, and scoped lessons. Measure both author acceptance/edit distance and engagement on comparable accepted posts; neither metric is sufficient alone.
  6. Run two narrow spikes: Graphiti versus relational+filtered-vector retrieval for temporal preference recall; Phoenix versus Langfuse for delayed feedback/outcome linkage. Keep both outside the canonical evidence model.

Deferred deliberately

  • Fully autonomous self-reflection/prompt rewriting.
  • Global cross-workflow preferences without a declared scope and evidence threshold.
  • Graph database adoption before relationship queries demonstrate value.
  • Using engagement as a training target rather than one noisy outcome feature.
  • Any LinkedIn member-feed polling or scraping without explicit official authorization.

10. Method, coverage, and sources

Coverage ledger and stopping rule

The research floor was 28 named implementation options; this memo evaluates 29: 6 durable runtimes, 7 memory/context systems, 5 retrieval/knowledge layers, 5 trace/evaluation systems, 4 research-derived patterns, and 2 official LinkedIn access surfaces. The categories intentionally overlap because some candidates compose across layers.

Discovery used a capability-first search lattice: synonyms for memory (episodic, semantic, procedural, temporal graph, persistent context), learning (reflection, verbal feedback, experience replay, preference inference), durability (workflow, checkpoint, signal, human-in-the-loop), and evaluation (traces, feedback labels, datasets, experiments). It then checked primary documentation/source code, cited/backlinked research, integration pages, and platform policy documentation. Candidates that were purely model wrappers or lacked a clear fit to a layer were not elevated to the comparison table.

Stopping rule: not “we found 29 names.” Each layer has several materially different approaches; candidate claims are grounded in primary sources; and remaining uncertainty is stated as an experiment or access check. The known blind spots are closed-source internal systems, non-English literature, and vendor capabilities not publicly documented. Those are disclosed rather than treated as absence.
